Rogue Potato // Token Impersonation · Red

local service (SeImpersonate) → nt authority\system

A step-by-step map of the Rogue Potato privilege-escalation attack — the one that turns a service account into SYSTEM by stealing SYSTEM's own token. The fiddliest attack in the Windows PrivEsc room, laid out piece by piece. Generic methodology from a public lab — nothing about the real PNPT exam (NDA).

i The idea

Some accounts hold SeImpersonatePrivilege — a “badge” that lets a process wear the security token of anything that authenticates to it. It is usually held by service accounts (local service, network service, IIS, SQL), not normal users. Rogue Potato tricks a SYSTEM process into authenticating to you, then puts on its SYSTEM token — so you climb up to SYSTEM. The trigger to remember: whoami /priv shows SeImpersonatePrivilege → “Potato → SYSTEM.”

ii The flow

🐉 Kali (attacker)

socat redirectorlistens on :135, forwards to target :9999 — the bridge the fake OXID resolver needs
↓ OXID redirect (135 → 9999)
nc :4444catches the local-service shell
nc :5555catches the SYSTEM shell 👑

🪟 Target (victim)

Elevated admin cmdhigh-integrity prompt (Run as administrator)
↓ PsExec64 -u "local service"
local service shellholds SeImpersonatePrivilege — reverse.exe → Kali:4444
↓ run RoguePotato.exe from here
SYSTEM shellreverse2.exe launched with SYSTEM token → Kali:5555 👑
Why socat? RoguePotato needs an OXID resolver on port 135, but you can't easily listen there on the target. So the exploit points at your Kali on 135, and socat forwards that to port 9999 on the target where RoguePotato is really listening. That's the whole redirect trick.
Why two ports? The local-service shell and the SYSTEM shell are two separate shells — each needs its own listener on its own port (4444 and 5555), or they collide. So you build two payloads: reverse.exe (4444) and reverse2.exe (5555).

iv Remediation

Remove SeImpersonate from service accounts where it isn't needed. Keep Windows patched (the OXID/DCOM coercion tricks get fixed over time). Run services under least-privilege identities, and watch for the Potato tool family on endpoints.
